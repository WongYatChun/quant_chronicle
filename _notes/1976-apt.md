## Why?

Must multifactor pricing require a complete model of investor preferences and equilibrium?

## Who?

Stephen Ross, *The Arbitrage Theory of Capital Asset Pricing* (1976).

## How?

Assume a factor structure:

$$
R=\mu+BF+\epsilon,\qquad E[F]=0.
$$

A diversified portfolio with zero investment and zero factor exposure satisfies

$$
\mathbf1^\top w=0,\qquad B^\top w=0,\qquad w^\top\epsilon\approx0.
$$

Its return is approximately $w^\top\mu$. Under the appropriate large-market no-arbitrage conditions, such portfolios cannot systematically offer a positive, nearly riskless payoff. This implies an approximate pricing relation:

$$
\boxed{\mu_i-r_f\approx\beta_i^\top\lambda}
$$

when a risk-free asset exists. Otherwise the intercept is a zero-beta return.

## So what?

APT motivates multiple priced exposures without CAPM's specific market-portfolio equilibrium. It does not identify which factors to use.

$$
\text{Common return variation}\;\not\Rightarrow\;\text{nonzero risk premium}.
$$

Classical APT is an approximate large-market restriction, not a guarantee that every finite portfolio has zero pricing error.

**Research connection:** Fama–French constructs observable factor portfolios from firm characteristics.
